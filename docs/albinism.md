# From Theophany to Pathology: The Symbolic Semantics of Extreme Bodily Whiteness in English Literary Culture

## Project Concept

This project investigates the changing symbolic meanings of extreme bodily whiteness in English literary culture from the early modern period to the nineteenth century, while situating English traditions within their biblical and broader European intellectual inheritance.

Rather than taking the modern medical category of "albinism" as its starting point, the project examines the historically recurring physiognomic complex of unusually white, extraordinary pallor, and, in some representations, unusual eyes. The central question is how such visible characteristics acquired different symbolic meanings across literary, religious, scientific and broader cultural contexts.

The project treats its comparative material - classical, Old Norse, Old English, and Central/South-Eastern European - under two distinct relations to the English and biblical corpus that forms its principal object of study, and does not conflate them. Material with a documented reception history within the period under study (for example, nineteenth-century Eddic translation and medievalist reception) is treated as primary evidence for the English tradition itself. Material with no such demonstrable channel of transmission or influence is treated as comparative and heuristic: adduced to sharpen, by contrast or structural parallel, what is historically specific to the English case, without any claim of descent, borrowing or equivalence. Each comparative body of material introduced below is identified according to which of these two relations applies.

The project begins with the theophanic imagery of the "Son of Man" in Revelation 1 as one important and influential articulation of extreme bodily whiteness within the Christian tradition. It does not, however, assume a simple or continuous line of descent from biblical imagery to later representations. Instead, it asks how subsequent literary, theological, scientific and cultural traditions reworked, transformed, reinterpreted or departed from earlier symbolic complexes.

This central biblical articulation does not stand alone even within its own tradition. Related imagery appears in the Hebrew Bible and Second Temple literature that shaped it and ran alongside it - the "Ancient of Days" of Daniel 7, whose hair is "like pure wool," supplies the imagery Revelation 1 itself draws on directly, while the account of Noah's birth in 1 Enoch 106 offers an earlier and notably more ambivalent treatment of the same physiognomic complex: white hair, luminous eyes and radiant skin here provoke not recognition of divinity but paternal terror and suspicion of illegitimate, hybrid or angelic parentage. This apocryphal material is valuable to the project precisely because, on the reading the project intends to test, it suggests that extreme bodily whiteness carried unstable and contested meanings from a very early point in its textual history, rather than acquiring ambivalence only through later secularisation or medicalisation.

A second, distinct body of comparative material derives from classical ethnography and paradoxography. Pliny the Elder and Julius Solinus record traditions of a white-haired, pale-eyed people inhabiting ancient Caucasian Albania, whose appearance was read variously as omen, wonder and portent. This material situates extreme bodily whiteness within a second, non-theological framework of the marvellous and the anomalous, comparative in the sense set out above.

The project also draws, more cautiously, on comparative material from outside the English and biblical traditions altogether: the Old Norse corpus, in which whiteness and brightness are bound up with the god Baldr and with a documented Old Norse colour-vocabulary distinguishing shades of white, black and red; and Central and South-Eastern European folk belief, particularly Hungarian and Balkan traditions of fairies (tündér) and related supernatural figures, as studied by Éva Pócs. Its function here, too, is comparative and heuristic in the sense already set out: examining structurally similar or contrasting treatments of extreme whiteness in adjacent symbolic systems sharpens the historical specificity of what is, and is not, distinctive about the English literary and biblical material that remains the project's principal object of study.

## Positioning within Existing Scholarship

Thomas White's 2012 University of Manchester doctoral thesis *'Their Whiteness Is Not Like Ours': A Social and Cultural History of Albinism and Albino Identities, 1650–1914* provides an important historiographical foundation for research into the social, medical and racial construction of albinism in the early modern and modern periods. White traces the emergence of albinism as a medico-scientific category through travel writing, natural history, medical classification, heredity theory and racial discourse.

The present project builds upon this scholarship while asking a different primary question. Rather than investigating principally the historical construction of albinism itself, it examines the longer history of the cultural semantics surrounding extreme bodily whiteness and asks how an older physiognomic and lexical complex - including white hair, unusually pale skin and luminous or fiery eyes - interacted with, influenced, or was transformed by the emergence of medical, racial and literary discourses surrounding albinism and visibly marked bodies.

The project has also been informed by Charlotte Baker's scholarship on the literary representation of albinism in Francophone contexts and on the cultural meanings and representations attached to albinism. Baker's work is valuable to the present project for two distinct reasons, beyond the general fact of shared subject matter. First, her material - situated primarily in East and Central African contexts where albinism has been the object of ritual violence, human-rights advocacy and contested local cosmologies of witchcraft and misfortune - demonstrates concretely that the symbolic meaning of extreme bodily whiteness is not a transhistorical or culturally universal property of the phenotype itself, but is produced locally, within specific discursive and social conditions. This comparative demonstration usefully disciplines the present project's own historical claims: it is a warning against treating the English and biblical material as expressing some default or natural human response to whiteness, and a reminder that the symbolic meanings recovered here must be shown to be actively constructed within English literary, theological and scientific discourse rather than assumed as intuitively available.

Secondly, Baker's grounding in critical disability studies offers a methodological model for keeping the lived, embodied dimension of albinism in view even where the present project's primary evidence is textual and symbolic rather than ethnographic or testimonial. A purely semantic or literary-historical history of "extreme bodily whiteness" risks treating the phenotype as no more than a signifier available for appropriation by theology, natural history or fiction, dissolving the actual bodies of historically albino people into the symbolic economies constructed around them. Baker's insistence on stigma, embodiment and the social consequences of visible difference as continuous concerns - rather than as a separate question from representation - provides a check against that risk, and connects the project's textual-historical work back to the same disability-studies commitments that inform its reflexive methodology below. Although the principal corpus of the proposed research is English, the project necessarily recognises that English literary culture developed within wider European, biblical and intellectual traditions.

The project's engagement with Old Norse and Old English material falls on both tracks of the distinction set out above. The Eddic translations, antiquarianism and medievalist enthusiasm associated with figures such as William Morris form a genuine channel through which Norse imagery entered English literary culture within the project's own period, and this material is accordingly treated as primary reception evidence rather than comparanda. Earlier or untranslated Old Norse and Old English material - including Anglo-Saxon traditions of supernatural whiteness and otherness, as studied by Alaric Hall - falls instead on the comparative track, alongside the classical and Central European material discussed above. The project draws on the scholarship and methodology of Hall and of Jackson Crawford, including Crawford's dissertation research on the semantics of colour terminology - black, red and white - in Old Norse-Icelandic literature, for this second, comparative strand of the engagement.

The reception of Norse whiteness-symbolism was not confined to nineteenth-century medievalism. The same imagery was later appropriated within twentieth-century racial-nationalist and occult movements, most notably in Nazi-era mysticism; this later history is beyond the scope of the present project, which concerns English literary culture to the nineteenth century, but its existence is acknowledged here as a documented further stage in the reception trajectory the project otherwise traces only as far as Morris and his contemporaries (see, e.g., Goodrick-Clarke's work on the occult roots of Nazism, and Van Deusen's work on Old Norse reception history).

A further point of distinction lies in methodology. Existing scholarship has necessarily depended primarily upon selective reading of individual texts and bodies of material. By combining computational historical semantics with traditional literary-historical methods, this project seeks to identify large-scale changes in symbolic association and semantic environment across several centuries of English textual culture before subjecting those patterns to detailed historical and literary interpretation.

The computational component is therefore not an end in itself, nor is it intended simply to generate additional examples. Its purpose is to address questions of symbolic continuity, transformation, divergence and reception at a scale that conventional methods alone cannot easily sustain.

## Methodology Note: The Recording of Reaction

A central methodological problem in investigating historical representations of albinism and extreme bodily whiteness - set out as a research question below (RQ4) - is that the surviving textual record may disproportionately preserve reactions to the phenotype rather than the ordinary experience of encountering it. Testing this requires treating reaction as an independent textual variable rather than as an inherent property of the phenotype.

The project proposes to classify occurrences of extreme bodily whiteness according to two dimensions: first, whether the text records an explicit reaction to the phenotype at all; and second, where a reaction is present, its character and intensity (astonishment, veneration, fear, disgust, theological or medical interpretation, and so on). Particular attention will be given to occurrences in which extreme whiteness is described in apparently matter-of-fact terms, without accompanying affective, evaluative, supernatural, medical, racial or otherwise interpretive response, since these are the cases most likely to be underrepresented by ordinary processes of textual survival and most valuable for testing the selection effect directly.

This annotation scheme separates two questions that the corpus analysis is able to address with different degrees of confidence. How frequently extreme whiteness is associated with recorded reaction in surviving texts is directly accessible to corpus analysis. How likely an encounter was to be recorded at all when it produced no remarkable reaction can only be approached indirectly, since the unrecorded population of such encounters is not available for study; here the project can only reason from the shape and density of the matter-of-fact cases it does find. The distinction between these two questions is treated as methodologically load-bearing throughout, rather than as a caveat to be noted once and set aside: it governs how the project interprets frequency counts derived from the computational corpus, and cautions specifically against treating the prevalence of highly charged reactions in the surviving record as evidence that such reactions were typical of the population of encounters as a whole.

## Research Questions

  phenotype >> representation >> symbolic type >> recurring configuration

*(The schema above traces the analytic path the project follows for each occurrence identified in the corpus: from a described physical phenotype, to its textual representation, to the symbolic type that representation instantiates or resists, to the recurring cross-textual configurations that emerge at scale. RQ1–3 address the first three stages historically; RQ4 addresses a confound affecting all three; RQ5 addresses the fourth stage computationally.)*

### 1. Construction and change

How is extreme bodily whiteness symbolically constructed in English literary culture between the early modern period and the nineteenth century, and how do its semantic associations change across this period? Detailed attention will be given to the transmission history of material drawn from the earliest periods covered. For instance, the account of Noah's birth in 1 Enoch 106 was not available in English before Richard Laurence's 1821 translation of the Ethiopic text recovered by James Bruce in 1773, and must be situated accordingly - distinct from the partially overlapping but textually separate Book of the Watchers material, which reached an English readership considerably earlier via Samuel Purchas's 1613 translation of the Greek fragments Joseph Scaliger had published from Syncellus in 1606. The project treats such transmission facts as constraining what can be claimed about the availability of specific imagery to English writers at a given date, rather than treating "Enoch" as a single undifferentiated source available throughout the period.

### 2. Discursive interaction and medicalisation

How do religious, literary, medical and scientific discourses interact in shaping representations of the body of 'the other', specifically examining extreme bodily whiteness, and under what historical conditions do symbolic representations become associated with actual bodily conditions, particularly albinism?

### 3. Continuity and comparison

What continuities, transformations and discontinuities can be identified between earlier theological and literary representations of extreme bodily whiteness and later medicalised understandings of bodily difference, and how do structurally comparable complexes in adjacent traditions illuminate what is historically specific to the English case?

### 4. Selection, salience and the mundane phenotype

Textual survival is not neutral with respect to reaction: encounters with extreme bodily whiteness that provoked astonishment, fear, veneration or interpretation are structurally more likely to have been recorded than encounters regarded as unremarkable. This raises a question that precedes any claim about the symbolic meaning of whiteness itself - whether the corpus preserves evidence of mundane, non-reactive encounters with the phenotype at all, and if so, how such depictions compare with those in which whiteness functions as a symbolic type. Distinguishing a documented absence of reaction from an undocumented population of unremarkable encounters is methodologically crucial, since the latter can only be approached indirectly (see Methodology Note above).

### 5. Diachronic semantic search

Can a reverse time-bracketed embedding search anchored on the modern term *albino* reveal recurring physiognomically similar historical types - such as the Ancient of Days, Son of Man, and Baldr - without presupposing their equivalence?

## Researcher Position and Reflexivity

This project concerns the historical representation of albinism and extreme bodily whiteness and is undertaken by a researcher who himself has albinism. This relationship to the subject informs the choice of research questions and creates a particular awareness of the potential consequences of historical representation. It does not, however, determine the meanings that the research seeks to identify.

The project does not assume that albinism, extreme bodily whiteness or the people represented through these categories possess any inherent symbolic, moral or cultural value. Its object is instead historical: to investigate the meanings that particular writers, communities and discursive traditions have attached to visibly marked bodies.

The project will therefore adopt an explicitly reflexive methodology. Interpretative claims will be grounded wherever possible in textual, linguistic and historical evidence, while distinctions will be maintained between evidence, scholarly interpretation and the researcher's own critical judgement. The aim is not to claim complete objectivity, but to ensure methodological transparency and critical awareness of the processes through which interpretation is produced.
